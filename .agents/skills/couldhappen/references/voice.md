# Voice: making characters talk like people

A method for dialogue that sounds spoken. Read it before writing an entry with speech, and before a new character's first line. It is craft: it says nothing about what may happen, only how it lands on the page. Adapt it to the story; a stylised genre may want some polish back.

## What goes wrong

- Every character gets a finished, quotable line, every turn.
- Every line answers the previous line too neatly, like a tennis rally.
- Nobody fumbles, repeats themselves by accident, or says something boring.
- Nobody is doing anything with their hands while talking.
- The narration explains how charming the line was, which makes it feel performed.
- Two characters have the same wit with different vocabulary.

## The rules

1. **Everyone wants something small and practical.** Open the door. Get the shower. Not owe a favour. End the conversation. The line is the surface of that want, not a speech about it.
2. **Start with the boring fact.** The key, the phone battery, the time. Feelings come second, or not at all.
3. **Let people answer the wrong question.** A question can be met with another question, a fact, a look, or nothing. Not every line is a reply to the last line.
4. **One imperfection per turn of speech.** A false start, a repeated word, a mundane detail, a sentence that trails off. Not all four, and not in every line.
5. **Cut every aphorism.** If a line could go on a poster, flatten it. A line survives if it is a transaction, not a joke.
6. **Break the symmetry.** One person talks more. One person says three words. Let a long, messy answer sit next to a short one. Do not alternate zingers.
7. **Put an action in the way.** They talk while looking for the key, wiping their hair, reading the sign, setting down the bottle. Action interrupts speech and speech interrupts action.
8. **Keep tags plain.** `says`, `asks`, `tells him`. No `quips`, `offers`, `breathes`, no adverb doing the acting. The line carries the tone.
9. **Stop explaining the line.** No narration after dialogue telling the reader how charming, dry, or devastating it was. If the line needs the explanation, the line is wrong.
10. **Read it out loud.** If it sounds like a table read, rewrite it. If you run out of breath where a person would not, rewrite it.
11. **The swap test.** If another character in the cast could say the line unchanged, it is not theirs. Rewrite until only they could say it.
12. **Natural is not shapeless.** Cut the boring parts. The sound should be real; the scene still moves.

## Voice cards

Give each recurring character a voice card in your private notes before their first real scene. The card is five short fields, not an adjective:

- **Rhythm.** Sentence length, pace, whether they start before they know the end.
- **Vocabulary.** The words they reach for, the ones they avoid, profanity, register.
- **Tic.** What they do instead of naming the feeling.
- **Silence.** What they do with a pause, and what their silence means.
- **Never says.** One thing they would never say, even under pressure.

An example card (make your own from the character):

> **Rhythm.** Short. Starts mid-thought. One long sentence when tired.
> **Vocabulary.** Work words, prices, "right", "fine". No slang for its own sake.
> **Tic.** Answers a question with a smaller true fact.
> **Silence.** Lets it stand. The waiting is the pressure.
> **Never says.** "Please", twice.

If the story uses game-master's cast cards, the card's `voice` field is this card compressed to one line: "fast, run-on, fills silence" is usable; "witty" is not. A character card imported by the Director often has example dialogue: read it for rhythm and vocabulary, and write the voice card from it.

## The pass (run it before saving an entry with speech)

1. Count the spoken turns. Is anyone getting a zinger every time? Cut one.
2. Does every line answer the line before it too neatly? Make one answer something else.
3. Is there a physical action interrupting the talk? Add one.
4. Is there one small imperfection in the exchange? If not, add one, once.
5. Is there an aphorism, or a line that explains the theme? Flatten it.
6. Swap test each line: could another character say it? Rewrite.
7. Read the exchange aloud. Cut what you stumble over for the wrong reason.

## Before and after

Before, a table read:

> "You're late," she says. "Let me guess. The train."
>
> "The train," he says.
>
> "Of course. The train is always the excuse."

After:

> "You're late." She doesn't look up from the sink. "Let me guess. Train."
>
> "Train."
>
> "Mm." She turns the tap off, wipes her hands on her jeans. "There's cold pasta. Or the bin."

The second version keeps the same information and the same irritation. It loses the polish: the interrupted greeting, the one-word answer, the action in the middle of the speech, and the practical fact instead of the feeling.

## Delegated lines

If you have characters' lines drafted by subagents (the character-brains package does this), their output is material, not a script. Ask for one to three short lines, give the voice card and what the character's hands are doing, and run the pass over the assembled scene yourself.
